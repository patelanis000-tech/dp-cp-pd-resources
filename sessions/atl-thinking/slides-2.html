Thinking Skills

What Does Thinking Look Like in Different Subjects?

Mathematics

Choose and justify a method, compare approaches, explain why a result makes sense, and use a familiar idea in an unfamiliar problem.

Sciences

Analyse evidence, evaluate explanations, question assumptions, design procedures and decide whether evidence supports a conclusion.

Languages

Interpret meaning, consider alternative readings or viewpoints, construct arguments and reflect on how language choices affect meaning.

Individuals & Societies

Evaluate claims, weigh evidence, compare explanations, identify limitations and make reasoned judgments.

Arts

Generate possibilities, make creative choices, explain decisions, revise work and reflect on how ideas develop.

TOK / Core

Question how knowledge is produced, examine assumptions, reflect on process and connect learning across subjects and contexts.

Core Thinking Skills

Critical thinking

Analyse information, evaluate evidence, question assumptions and make reasoned judgments.

Creative thinking

Generate possibilities, make new connections and approach problems flexibly.

Reflection

Think carefully about learning, process and outcomes before, during and after learning.

Metacognition

Think about how you learn. Notice which strategies you are using and decide whether they are actually helping.

The IB ATL guidance places particular emphasis on metacognition, reflection and critical thinking, alongside creative thought and related dispositions.

Developing Thinking Skills

Transfer of learning

Can students use what they learned when the situation looks different?

Students take knowledge or a strategy from one context and apply it in a new, unfamiliar or independent context.

Self-regulation

Can students set a goal, check their progress and change what they are doing?

Students monitor their own learning instead of waiting for the teacher to tell them every next step.

Mental moves

What are the actual steps inside “analyse”, “evaluate” or “critique”?

Complex thinking becomes easier to teach when the hidden steps are made explicit.

Visible thinking

Can students show their reasoning, not only the final answer?

When thinking is visible, students and teachers can examine it, discuss it and improve it.

Where Do Students Struggle?

Memorizing instead of analysing

Students can repeat an explanation without being able to make a judgment or apply it independently.

Transfer

They can complete a familiar task but struggle when the question, context or presentation changes.

Dependence on the teacher

Students wait for prompts, confirmation or the next step instead of monitoring their own learning.

Superficial reflection

Reflection can become a routine answer rather than an honest examination of process and strategy.

Familiarity feels like learning

Seeing a worked answer or reading feedback can create the feeling of understanding.

Confidence without evidence

Students may feel sure they understand without checking whether they can do it independently.

A question for students

How do I know I have learned it?

Seeing the answer ≠ learning
Recognizing ≠ recalling
Following ≠ doing independently
Familiar question ≠ transfer
Can I do it without looking?
Can I explain why it works?
Can I do it again later?
Can I use it when the question looks different?
